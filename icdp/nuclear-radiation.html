Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation

Try this: Choose a source and put paper, aluminium or lead between it and the detector. Look at the ions (+ and −) each radiation leaves in the air. Then see how the radiations bend in a field, and build decay equations in which the nucleon number and proton number balance.

How decay changes the nucleus Supplement

α decay: nucleon number A − 4, proton number Z − 2
β decay: A unchanged, Z + 1 (a neutron changes into a proton and an electron)
γ emission: A and Z unchanged (the nucleus loses energy)

Readings

Properties Core

α: helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons), charge +2. Strongly ionising. Stopped by paper or a few cm of air.
β (β⁻): fast electron from the nucleus, charge −1. Moderately ionising. Stopped by a few mm of aluminium.
γ: electromagnetic radiation, no charge. Weakly ionising. Reduced, not stopped, by several cm of lead or thick concrete.

Safety precautions Core

Ionising radiation can kill cells, cause mutations and lead to cancer. To reduce the hazard:

Reduce the time of exposure.
Increase the distance: handle sources with tongs, at arm's length, never point them at people.
Use shielding: keep sources in lead-lined boxes, labelled and locked away, and return them straight after use.

The most ionising radiation (α) is the most dangerous inside the body, but the least dangerous outside it because skin and air absorb it.
